# Escape Game Design

Design educational escape games that run in a normal classroom, physically or as a tabletop or hybrid kit. The output is a scenario plus a station-by-station breakdown: puzzle, solution, materials, hint, and the content each station teaches.

## Design stance

- "Escape" is optional. Defusing a bomb, finding an artifact, decoding a message, or preventing a disaster all work. What matters is a compelling goal under time pressure.
- Students discover content, they are not told it. Every puzzle must require engaging with the target content. If it can be solved without the subject, it is decoration.
- Use competitive cooperation: teams cooperate internally and compete against other teams. Research on classroom escape games shows this beats pure cooperation or pure competition.
- Teams of 4-5. Fewer gives too few ideas, more invites social loafing.
- First design: plan 30 minutes. Warm-ups are 15, deep dives 60.
- After a few single-subject games, go interdisciplinary.

## Station rules

1. First station is easy. It builds confidence and buys motivation for the harder ones.
2. Difficulty rises station by station toward a satisfying climax.
3. Every solution leads logically to the next challenge. Players should feel each discovery brings them closer to the goal.
4. One or more puzzles or coded messages per station, each tied to content.
5. Every station has a hint prepared in advance, delivered in-narrative (a telegraph from your mentor, a note from the archivist). Consider a hint budget: three hints per team, they choose when to spend them.
6. The teacher can enter the story as a character to nudge, never to give the answer. Some struggle is the learning.
7. Instructions are unambiguous. A station that drags makes teams give up.

## Puzzle toolbox

- Jigsaw pieces that assemble into a message the team must read carefully.
- UV pen and blacklight. Leave the pen and light near the first hidden message, then let curiosity drive the rest.
- Ciphers: Morse and Braille for beginners, pigpen and custom symbol sets later.
- Math where the result is the lock code. Anagrams and word searches with embedded messages. Logic grids and sequence puzzles.
- Encoded links: a puzzle whose answer is the tail of a video URL.
- Locks: 3-4 digit combination for numbers, letter lock for words, direction lock for sequences, key lock for search tasks, multi-lock hasp for the final challenge.
- QR codes, password-protected files, Google Forms as digital locks. Always keep a non-digital Plan B.

## Worked example, kindergarten (ages 5-6)

Topic shapes and numbers, two class periods, 20 students in groups of 4-5. Phase 1: each group gets outline cards and loose sticks, forms the shapes, and teacher approval unlocks phase 2. Phase 2: progressive lock stations, each opened by a shape or number challenge, with hint cards for stuck teams, and each success reveals part of a final message.

## Before running it

Test the whole thing yourself and time it. If the designer cannot solve a puzzle in 5 minutes, it is too hard. No heavy, fragile or hazardous props. Prepare a debrief discussion; much of the learning consolidates there.

## Anti-patterns

- Puzzles unconnected to content or story.
- Stations one strong student can solve alone.
- A design that dies when the wifi does.
- Over-hinting that removes the struggle.
- Puzzles without a story, which are worksheets with extra steps.

## Done when

A facilitator could set up the stations from the materials list, run the game in the planned time, and every puzzle solved means content was learned.
