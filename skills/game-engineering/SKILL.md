# Game Engineering

The motivational backbone behind the other four skills. Use it to diagnose a system that is not working, to design the reward and engagement architecture of a new one, or to balance an existing one. Mechanics are the plate and fork; the emotion is the meal.

## Design stance

- External rewards activate behavior short term, and the behavior stops when the reward stops. The design's job is to migrate motivation from external to internal. Gamification is the bridge, not the destination.
- Never reward an already intrinsically motivated behavior. It destroys the motivation.
- Autonomy, mastery and purpose are the three sources to build on. Voluntary entry into the game, visible progress toward a goal with harder tasks as skill grows, and a purpose bigger than the individual (even "rescue the princess" counts).
- Balance white-hat drives (meaning, creativity, social) with black-hat ones (scarcity, unpredictability, loss). Kahoot's countdown and visible answer counts keep even the students who cannot answer in the flow.

## Rewards

Rank rewards by durability: **status** (titles, roles, recognition), then **access** (do what others cannot), then **power** (choose teammates, set the next challenge), and last **stuff**. A good status reward requires the behavior it rewards: the Reading Champion picks the next class story and reads it first.

Never hand out a reward casually. Even a shared meal is presented with ceremony: in an envelope, announced, celebrated. Casual delivery devalues it.

Delivery options: fixed on completion, random spin, unexpected surprise, raffle tickets for a long-term behavior, rewards that must be shared with someone, and collection sets where completing the set pays out.

## Three-phase journey

1. Onboarding: individual, easy, personal goals set to the player's level. A step app starts with 5,000 steps, not a leaderboard.
2. Scaffolding: social comparison and cooperation appear, goals adjust to demonstrated ability.
3. Endgame: players contribute, mentor, lead, and get advanced challenges.

The classroom rule that follows: if one student finishes all five units while others are on unit one, the game is broken. Require the fast student to help a peer finish unit one before unlocking their own unit two. Everyone arrives together.

## Player types

Every design needs something for achievers (progression, ranks), explorers (hidden content, multiple paths), socializers (teams, mentoring, status) and competitors (tournaments, ranked play). Designing only for achievers loses most of the room.

## Feedback

Make feedback juicy: immediate, inviting, repeatable, coherent with the story, continuous rather than checkpoint-only, and occasionally surprising. Compare "You failed, try again" with "Your historic first attempt, and history never forgets firsts. Ready to beat your record?"

## Diagnosing a broken system

Check, in order: is participation voluntary; does the first phase start easy and personal; does challenge track skill or has it stalled at boredom or frustration; which player types have nothing to do; are rewards status and access or mostly stuff; has any reward been inflated by handing it out for everything; is there an endgame for players who max out; has the system been updated since launch.

## Anti-patterns

- Rewarding what students already love doing.
- Dropping players straight into competition.
- Nothing left to do at max level.
- A reward for every action.
- Non-game content that interrupts play.
- A design that never changes after launch.

## Done when

The design names its motivational sources, serves all four player types, has a three-phase journey, a reward ladder that leans on status and access, and a stated plan for measuring and adjusting.
